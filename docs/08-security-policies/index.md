# Security Policies

The short, usable policies a SOC relies on, written in plain language so people actually read and follow them. These are starting templates with `[ORGANISATION]` placeholders. Adapt them, get them approved, and keep them to a page or two each. A policy nobody reads protects nobody.

!!! abstract "At a glance"
    - Audience: SOC manager, CISO, IT, HR, and every employee for the guide
    - Time to read: 5 minutes
    - Outcome: you know which policies matter most and have drafts to adapt

!!! warning "These are templates, not legal advice"
    Every placeholder in brackets must be filled in for your organisation, and every policy reviewed by whoever owns risk and law where you operate. Use these as a fast start, not a finished document.

## Why a SOC cares about policy

A SOC detects and responds, but policy decides what "wrong" means and what the SOC is allowed to do about it. Policy gives the SOC its authority and its boundaries.

| Without policy | With policy |
|---|---|
| The SOC blocks an account and is accused of overreach | The SOC acts within an agreed, approved mandate |
| "Is this allowed?" is argued during every incident | The answer is written down in advance |
| People do not know how to report a problem | There is one clear, blameless way to report |
| Weak passwords and shared logins are nobody's fault | There is a standard to detect against and enforce |

## Keep policies short and alive

The most common policy mistake is writing a fifty-page document that is approved once and never read again.

- [ ] One page or two per policy. If it is longer, people will not read it.
- [ ] Plain language. Write for the people who must follow it, not for auditors.
- [ ] Say what to do and what not to do, with the why in one line.
- [ ] Name an owner and a review date on every policy.
- [ ] Review yearly, and after any incident that the policy touched.

A short policy people follow beats a long one they ignore. Start with the few here, get them approved, and build from there.
